How Accurate Are Pregnancy Due Date Calculators?

Understanding the science (and art) of predicting when your baby will arrive.

If you've used an online pregnancy calculator or asked your doctor, "When is my due date?", you've probably noticed they all give you a single date. You circle it on the calendar, plan your maternity leave around it, and buy baby clothes sized for that arrival window. But here's the truth: due dates are estimates, not guarantees.

In fact, studies show that only about 4% of babies are born on their exact due date. About 80% arrive within two weeks before or after that date. So how accurate are these calculators, really?

How Online Calculators Work

Most digital pregnancy calculators use Naegele's Rule: take your last menstrual period (LMP), add 280 days (40 weeks), and that's your estimated due date. This assumes:

  • You have a regular 28-day cycle
  • Ovulation occurred on day 14
  • Conception happened exactly two weeks after your LMP started

If these assumptions are true, the estimate is accurate within about 7 days. But for many women, especially those with irregular cycles or who don't track ovulation, the margin of error is much wider.

Factors That Reduce Accuracy

Several variables can shift your actual due date away from the LMP-based estimate:

1. Irregular Menstrual Cycles

If your cycles vary by more than a few days, LMP dating becomes unreliable. Women with PCOS, thyroid disorders, or perimenopause often have irregular cycles, making the "day 14 ovulation" assumption incorrect.

2. Late Ovulation

Even with a "28-day cycle," ovulation doesn't always happen on day 14. Stress, illness, or travel can delay ovulation, meaning conception happened later—and your due date is earlier than calculated.

3. First Trimester Bleeding

Some women experience light spotting early in pregnancy that they mistake for a period. If this happens, your recorded LMP is actually about two weeks later than your true LMP, pushing your due date forward by two weeks.

4. Remembering the Wrong Date

Human memory is fallible. Studies suggest many women misremember the start date of their last period by several days. small errors compound into meaningful differences.

When Are Due Dates Most Accurate?

Due date estimates are most reliable when:

  • You conceived through IVF with known transfer dates
  • You have a first-trimester ultrasound (11-14 weeks) confirming gestational age
  • You used ovulation predictor kits and tracked intercourse timing precisely

The Ultraound Advantage

First-trimester ultrasounds are considered the gold standard for dating pregnancy. By measuring the "crown-rump length" (CRL) of the embryo, doctors can determine gestational age within 5-7 days of accuracymuch better than LMP-based estimates.

If your first-trimester ultrasound gives a date that's more than 7 days different from your LMP estimate, your doctor will likely "revise" your due date to match the ultrasound.

What "Term" Means

You'll hear doctors talk about "term" pregnancy, which doesn't mean your due date is exact:

  • Early term: 37-38 weeks
  • Full term: 39-40 weeks
  • Late term: 41 weeks
  • Post term: 42 weeks or more

Babies born at 39-40 weeks have the best outcomes. Being "overdue" past 41 weeks increases the risk of complications, which is why doctors often discuss induction around that time.

Real-World Accuracy

In research studies of first-time mothers:

  • LMP-based estimates are within 1 week of actual birth: ~50% of the time
  • LMP-based estimates are within 2 weeks: ~80% of the time
  • Ultrasound dating at 12 weeks: within 10 days for 95% of cases

Tips for the Most Accurate Estimate

  1. Try to pinpoint your LMP as accurately as possible
  2. Use ovulation predictor kits if you want better precision
  3. Schedule a first-trimester ultrasound if possible
  4. Use a calculator that adjusts for cycle length if your cycles aren't exactly 28 days
